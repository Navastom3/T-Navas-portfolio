I was born a builder. From Legos at 5, Robotics at 10, building my own PCs at 13, I've been fascinated by understanding how things work — and figuring out how to build them myself. Today, as an engineering student at UBC, I'm developing embedded hardware and firmware for competitive robotics. But that passion started long before university.

I became fascinated by the systems behind games—whether that was the physics, the game logic, weapon mechanics, and enemy patterns in games where you had to adapt your approach to each encounter. At the same time, I became obsessed with the hardware making it all possible. Comparing different PC builds and looking at the trade-offs between different processors and GPUs, optimizing graphics settings to get the best balance, and digging into my computer's BIOS to understand the system one level deeper could keep me fascinated for hours during the pandemic. That interest eventually led to my first job in 2022 at the same computer store where I had bought my components, where I learned to troubleshoot and repair computers and laptops.

During high school, I explored a range of interests outside engineering before returning to technical projects more seriously at university. And I found that same fascination again while building embedded systems—except now I get to create the logic myself, discover its limitations, and debug the interactions between hardware and software until the system works the way I want it to. What I enjoy most is taking something I understand in theory and trying to make it work in the real world. Things rarely behave exactly the way I expect them to, and figuring out why is usually where I learn the most—whether I'm working on something of my own or contributing to a larger engineering system.

STM32 / Embedded C / Firmware

STM32 BMS Firmware Simulator

A battery-management-system simulator built on an STM32 Nucleo using modular embedded C. The firmware integrates I²C voltage and temperature sensing, ADC input, PWM outputs, interrupts, UART debugging, and a state machine that responds to the battery's temperature conditions with power limits and safety states.

PCB Design / KiCad / Electronics

STM32 4-Layer PCB

A four-layer STM32-based PCB designed in KiCad. The board incorporates USB-C power input, protection circuitry, a low-dropout regulator, oscillator circuitry, and an STM32 microcontroller, progressing from schematic design through PCB layout, DRC, and fabrication-ready Gerber files.

DESIGN TEAM

UBC Thunderbots

Member of UBC Thunderbots' Electrical subteam, where I'm developing embedded hardware and firmware for autonomous RoboCup Small Size League soccer robots. My main focus is migrating the motor driver's communication interface from SPI to CAN FD. I'm also contributing to motor-driver testing and hardware validation through an external burn-in fixture that I'm currently designing.
